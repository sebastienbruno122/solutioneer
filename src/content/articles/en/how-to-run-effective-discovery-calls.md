Discovery is the single most decisive — and most commonly rushed — step in a presales cycle. A brilliant demo built on a poorly understood need almost never converts. Here's a concrete method for getting it right.

## Why discovery gets rushed

Many presales professionals, especially early in their careers, treat discovery as a formality to get through on the way to the "real" part: the demo. That's an expensive mistake. A generic demo, even a technically flawless one, built on a vague understanding of the need, produces the same outcome almost every time — a polite prospect who isn't convinced and doesn't follow up.

A thorough discovery call does the opposite: it lets you build a short, targeted demo that speaks directly to the prospect's actual decision criteria — and it often takes less prep time than a generic "just in case" demo covering everything.

## Three layers of information you actually need

Good discovery isn't just a list of desired features. It needs to cover three distinct layers:

### 1. The stated need

What the prospect says they want to solve. This is the easiest layer to get, and also the least reliable on its own — prospects often describe an assumed solution rather than the underlying problem.

### 2. Context and constraints

The existing technical environment, current tools, security or compliance constraints, internal deadlines. This layer is what lets you anticipate technical objections before they surface mid-demo.

### 3. The real decision criteria

Who actually decides, on what specific criteria, and through what internal process. This is the most commonly skipped layer — and the most decisive one. A need that's technically well covered can still go nowhere if the real decision process was never understood.

## Questions that actually move the needle

Rather than a generic checklist, here are question framings that genuinely dig into each layer:

- **To get past the stated need**: "What happens today if this problem doesn't get solved?" — this often reveals the real urgency, or the lack of it.
- **To map constraints**: "What are you using for this today, and what's working well about it?" — understanding what already works prevents you from proposing a solution that breaks useful habits.
- **To clarify the decision**: "Besides you, who else will be involved in the final decision?" — a simple question, often avoided for fear of sounding presumptuous, but one that structures the rest of the sales cycle.

## Common traps to avoid

**Confusing discovery with a pitch.** A discovery call where the presales engineer talks more than the prospect has failed, even if it "felt good" in the room. The goal at this stage is to listen, not to convince.

**Accepting a surface-level answer without probing.** When a prospect says "security matters to us," that's an opening, not a complete answer. Asking "what would make you confident the security posture is sufficient?" turns a generality into something you can actually act on.

**Jumping straight to a demo without confirming understanding.** Playing back what you heard before proposing next steps ("So if I'm hearing you right, your top priority is X, with a hard constraint on Y — is that accurate?") prevents building an entire demo on a misunderstanding.

## What it's worth remembering

A thorough discovery call isn't longer than a shallow one — it's better aimed. The time saved on demo prep (fewer generic scenarios, more targeted ones) more than makes up for the time invested upfront. It's one of the rare cases in presales where slowing down at the start genuinely gets you there faster.
